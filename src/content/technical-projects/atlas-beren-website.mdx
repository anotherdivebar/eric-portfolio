## Make the platform understandable

The Atlas-Beren website is the public entry point to the business behind [ABS Tooling](/abs-tooling). It has to explain both the operating software and the help available around it: implementation, managed operations, and growth services.

My work connects the product narrative to the web implementation: page architecture, interactive demonstrations, reusable content models, responsive interfaces, and the inquiry flow. The site gives a visitor several ways into the same story—through their kind of business, a specific workflow, or a hands-on sample.

The central message follows the work itself: **win the customer, organize delivery, and understand what happened.** That makes a large platform easier to explore one useful task at a time.

## Let visitors try the handoffs

The guided demo follows a fictional customer through an estimate, delivery, invoice, and recorded payment. A small state model enforces the sequence: completing work, issuing an invoice, and recording a payment depend on the preceding state. Partial payments change the remaining balance, and the overview links the numbers back to the next action.

This is more useful than a gallery of disconnected screens. A buyer can see which record changes and why the next step becomes available. The sample remains in browser memory and resets on refresh; it does not call customer APIs or send anything to a real business.

I also built deeper demonstrations of CRM, estimating, scheduling, financial visibility, and field operations. The estimating workbench combines a local cost catalog, editable scope, exclusions, and a deterministic scope assistant. Suggestions start unchecked so the person reviewing the estimate chooses what belongs in it. Saved proposal snapshots carry context into the sample CRM and dispatch views.

The scheduling example makes dependencies and crew overlap visible. Downloadable sample PDFs illustrate customer-facing documents and are marked as samples. They are fixed examples, so the site does not imply that changing the demo generates a legally issued invoice or collects a payment.

## Give different buyers a clear route

Service businesses, project teams, and equipment or property operators approach the platform with different problems. Buyer paths connect those starting points to the relevant workflows. Additional business operations examples show how scheduling, purchasing, stock, production, and checks fit service work, light manufacturing, and restaurants.

The commercial structure now uses one inclusive platform subscription for all supported core modules and ten full users. Workspace activation is included; optional guided onboarding buys hands-on setup help. Custom implementation and managed services remain separate scopes. Pricing, onboarding, support, and data-handling pages put practical questions near the decision to inquire. The broader service architecture—Digital Foundation, Growth Engine, and Operations System—keeps the firm’s website, growth, and operational work connected without making every buyer read every service page.

Product pages and calls to action retain bounded context such as the selected workflow, audience, and inquiry source. A visitor who asks about maintenance can arrive at the form with that topic already identified. Invalid URL values fall back to known choices instead of becoming arbitrary form content.

## Make the offer concrete

The latest pricing pass addresses a practical buying question: what is included in access, and what extra work is someone agreeing to? A central offer definition keeps subscription terms and supported module descriptions aligned across pricing, product pages, onboarding, industry pages, and contextual inquiries.

The page separates ordinary software access from approved provider consumption and optional services. Managed-service descriptions explain the monthly tasks, the customer’s responsibilities, one-time work, and first-month totals. Advertising spend and other platform subscriptions stay explicit. Early-access availability and capacity still require confirmation.

## Make the inquiry useful

The form asks what the business is trying to connect, which tools it already uses, and where work gets stuck. Requests for a walkthrough can include preferred times and a time zone. These details give the person responding a useful starting point; submitting the form does not start a subscription or confirm an appointment.

The Next.js route checks the request origin, content type, payload size, and field values before calling Resend. Validation includes required fields, recognized interests, malformed addresses, oversized text, and a spam trap. The interface reports success only after the email provider accepts the request. An uncertain response gets an explicit unconfirmed state rather than a false success message.

Analytics use a defined set of events and allowed properties. Form descriptions and email addresses are excluded from those event payloads. Inquiry delivery remains a separate step from creating a customer record in ABS.

## Build a consistent experience

The site uses Next.js, React, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, and Framer Motion. Shared content definitions keep navigation, services, buyer paths, and product pages aligned as the offering develops. Canonical metadata, structured data, sitemap, and robots output support discovery and consistent links.

The visual system uses warm paper backgrounds, dark ink, gold accents, strong typography, and alternating sections. Long product pages mix short explanations, interactive examples, workflow lists, and focused calls to action. The same design language extends through pricing, industry pages, inquiries, and mobile layouts.
